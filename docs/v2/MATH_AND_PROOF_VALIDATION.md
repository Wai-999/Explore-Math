## Claims, assumptions and checked examples

- **Vectors and projection.** Coordinate addition/scaling derive from real-number laws. Projection onto a nonzero direction follows from residual orthogonality, with a separate numerical tiny-direction cutoff. Rescue fixtures: P=(1,2), T=(7,5), w=(2,−1) gives u=(4,4); P=(−2,1), T=(3,−1), w=(−1,2) gives u=(6,−4). Projection fixtures give p=(3,0) from u=(3,4), w=(2,0), and p=(3,3) from u=(4,2), w=(1,1); both residual dot products are zero.
- **Matrix action and determinant.** Linearity gives columns-as-basis-images. Normalized alternating bilinearity derives the 2D factor ad−bc; the displayed block map multiplies this by a separate z scale. General 3D volume is invoked as a theorem, not inferred from a mesh. The two worked maps give Av=(4,2,3), det=2, and Av=(−2,−2,3), det=−2. A determinant-one shear changes the length of e₂, disproving the stronger false guess.
- **Derivative and limits.** For nonzero h, the x² quotient simplifies to 2x+h. Its error from 2x is the absolute gap, so δ=ε establishes the quotient limit. The separate function-limit proof uses δ=min(1, ε/(2 abs(c)+1)). Worked secants are 9/2 at x=2,h=1/2 and −7/4 at x=−1,h=1/4, with derivatives 4 and −2. Zero h is undefined inside the quotient. Jump and cusp cases have unequal one-sided limits of the relevant function or quotient, respectively.
- **Gradient and optimization.** Partial differentiation gives ∇E=(x,2y), Hessian diag(1,2), and update factors 1−α and 1−2α. For this bowl, 0<α<1 contracts both coordinates and strictly decreases energy away from the minimizer. The two examples give next=(3/4,1/2), E=17/32 and next=(−9/5,2/5), E=89/50. At (0,1), α=1.2 raises E from 1 to 1.96. The two-valley origin has opposite curvature signs and arbitrarily nearby lower/higher values, establishing a saddle.

The formal rotation argument in the matrix path is valid: RᵀR=I implies ‖Rv‖²=vᵀRᵀRv=‖v‖². det(R)=+1 additionally specifies proper orientation; reflections can also preserve lengths. The inverse argument explicitly assumes invertibility. The gradient path's directional-derivative bridge assumes differentiability and a unit Euclidean direction; the quantity is a rate per unit distance. A nonzero gradient gives a direction of steepest increase; the zero gradient does not select a unique direction.

## Corrections made during author review

The implementation was corrected to reject sparse arrays; keep all generated rescue controls inside ±10 m; use the stable quadratic secant expression for tiny nonzero h; and make delta mode-specific, with no two-sided-limit delta at the jump. These regressions are now covered. A rejected surface step retains the prior point and reports the boundary, rather than silently clamping the mathematics.
